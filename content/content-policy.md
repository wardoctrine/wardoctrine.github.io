## Our Editorial Standard

War Doctrine publishes military history that is documentary, educational, and grounded in verified sources. Every editorial decision is made against a single test: **does this inform, or does it merely shock?**

These guidelines define what we publish, what we don't, and why.

---

## What We Cover

- Battles, campaigns, and military operations from all eras and all nations
- Decisions made by commanders, politicians, and ordinary soldiers under extreme conditions
- Forgotten units, overlooked individuals, and suppressed historical events
- The human cost of war — presented factually and with historical context
- Weapons, tactics, and strategy as historical subjects
- War crimes and atrocities when covered factually and with appropriate gravity

---

## What We Do Not Publish

**No glorification of violence or extremism**

Content will not celebrate, romanticize, or promote acts of political violence, genocide, terrorism, or extremist ideology. Historical figures and movements are described accurately — not celebrated.

**No graphic content for shock value**

Descriptions of violence serve the historical narrative. Gratuitous, detailed, or sensationalized depictions of suffering exist only to exploit — not to educate. We don't publish them.

**No dehumanizing language**

Historical events are described using the language of historical documentation. Slurs, dehumanizing characterizations, or language designed to demean any ethnic, religious, or national group are not used.

**No misinformation**

Claims are sourced. Where historical debate exists, it is acknowledged. We do not present fringe revisionism as established fact.

---

## How We Handle Difficult History

Some of the most important military history involves horrific events — the Holocaust, wartime massacres, the use of chemical weapons, colonial atrocities. War Doctrine does not avoid these subjects. Avoiding them would be a disservice to the historical record and to the people who suffered.

The standard we apply: cover it the way a serious historian would. Name what happened. Cite the evidence. Contextualize the scale and significance. Don't editorialize for emotional effect.

The Warsaw piece is an example of this approach — it documents deliberate destruction and mass loss without using that destruction as entertainment.
